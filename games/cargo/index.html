Why does a ship float?

A ship pushes water out of the way. The water pushes back up. She sinks until the water she has pushed aside weighs the same as she does. That is Archimedes' principle.

Every lorry you load makes her heavier, so she sinks a little deeper until she has pushed aside 44 more tonnes of water. Puffin Lass sinks about 1 cm for every 15 tonnes.

Sea water has salt in it, so a bucket of it is heavier than a bucket of river water. A ship floats higher in the sea. That is why the Plimsoll mark has an extra F line for fresh water.

Harbour master's job sheet

    MV Puffin Lass is made up. She is 120 m long and 22 m wide, and weighs 4,200 tonnes empty, including 200 tonnes of water in her anti-heel tanks. Vehicle weights are typical loaded weights; 44 tonnes is the most a six-axle lorry may weigh on UK roads.

    The close-up follows the shape set by the international load line rules: a ring 300 mm across with a line through it at the summer mark, lines 25 mm thick, and the marks for tropical (T), summer (S), winter (W), fresh water (F) and tropical fresh water (TF). The letters L R stand for the society that checked the marks. Ships longer than 100.5 m have no WNA (winter North Atlantic) line. The side views are drawn taller than life so you can see the ship move.
