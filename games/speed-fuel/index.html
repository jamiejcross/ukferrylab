Show me how

Planning backwards. You leave at 09:15 and must arrive by 11:45. That gives you 2 h 30 min, which is 2.5 hours. The crossing is 20 nautical miles, so over the sea bed you need 20 ÷ 2.5 = 8 knots. A knot is one nautical mile an hour.

The current is 2 knots against you, so the engines must make up for it: 8 + 2 = 10 knots. If the current were with you, you would take it away instead: 8 − 2 = 6 knots.

Choosing. Your minimum here is 10 knots. Test 10, 12 and 14 knots in the sea trials and pick the one with the shortest bar, as long as it is not marked late.

Working forwards. Say you choose 12 knots. Against a 2-knot current you make 12 − 2 = 10 knots over the sea bed. 20 ÷ 10 = 2 hours, so you arrive at 11:15. If the engines burn 300 litres an hour, the trip uses 300 × 2 = 600 litres.

Why does going fast use so much fuel?

Water pushes back on a moving ship. Go twice as fast and the water pushes back about four times as hard. You also cover the distance twice as quickly, so the engines need about eight times the power.

You get there in half the time, so for the whole trip a ship going twice as fast burns about four times the fuel.

But going very slowly is not free either. The lights, heating, kitchens and pumps use fuel every hour you are at sea. Somewhere in between is the best speed. Big cargo ships save fuel by sailing slowly on purpose. Sailors call this slow steaming.

Tides matter too. A current pushing you along is free speed. A current against you slows you down. The Pentland Firth, between Caithness and Orkney, has some of the strongest tides in Britain.

Scoring for each crossing: up to 100 for saving fuel (0 if you arrive late), 30 for planning backwards (10 for each of steps A, B and C), 30 for getting your arrival time right, and 10 for getting the fuel right. On Easy, step A is filled in for you and there is no fuel sum, so each crossing is out of 160. Harder hides the minimum-speed marker and gives you two sea trials instead of four.

Routes, distances, journey times and ships come from the Ferry Lab route finder. Timetable times include getting in and out of harbour, so you get the whole time for sailing. Departure times, extra time and currents are made up for the game, and the current is treated as flowing along the whole route.

Engine power uses the admiralty formula: power = (ship weight)2/3 × speed3 ÷ 450, with weight in tonnes and speed in knots. Where a ship's weight is unknown, it is estimated from its length. The power for lights, kitchens and heating is estimated from the number of passengers and cars, and is higher on overnight ships with cabins. Fuel is marine gas oil at 0.21 kg per kWh and 0.85 kg per litre, giving 3.2 kg of CO₂ for every kg burned. Passengers on board are 60% of the ship's capacity. Catriona and Hallaig are diesel-electric hybrids with batteries charged from the shore; the game works out their fuel as if they ran on diesel alone. Electricity for a home uses an average of about 7.4 kWh a day. Not made by or connected with any ferry company.
