Under the bonnet

What the computer works out

The sea is 32 waves added together
Each has its own length, speed and direction. Their sizes follow JONSWAP, a recipe scientists made from measuring real North Sea waves. Bigger seas get longer waves, and the crests get sharper.
Every slice of hull is pushed up
The hull is cut into 132 upright slices. Each is pushed up by the weight of the water it pushes aside. That's Archimedes' principle, worked out 120 times a second.
The ship fights back with its weight
This model ship weighs about 11,000 tonnes. Left alone, it would roll from side to side once every 12 seconds. When the waves arrive at the same rhythm, the rolling builds up.
Stabilisers add drag
Fins stick out from the hull under the water and resist the rolling. Switch them off to see the difference.

Experiments

Try this

  1. Set the waves to 5 metres from the side. Watch the roll number, then switch the stabilisers off. How big does the roll get?
  2. Change to "Straight ahead". Does the ship roll or pitch more now?
  3. Push the waves to 15 metres from straight ahead. Watch the front of the ship crash down and throw up spray.
  4. Watch "Biggest wave". How much bigger than the slider does it get?
  5. Go "On deck" in an 8 metre sea and watch the horizon.

The ship is a computer model with about the size and shape of MV Hjaltland, which sails this route: 125 m long, 20 m wide and 5.4 m deep in the water. Its colours are made up. This page is not made by or connected with NorthLink Ferries.

Simplified: the water pushes the ship up and down but not sideways, and spray is drawn as particles rather than worked out as flowing water. Real decisions about sailing are made by the ship's captain.
